Midnight Blizzard vs Microsoft (2024)

A nation-state actor read Microsoft's leadership mailboxes without touching a single endpoint. Mapping the intrusion to 13 ATT&CK techniques shows why cloud identity, not the person, is the weak point.

ProgrammeMSc · Advanced Incident Response
FrameworkMITRE ATT&CK Enterprise v19.2
Scope13 techniques · 10 tactics
Key insightWhole chain → 3 identity log sources

The incident in one line

Between late November 2023 and January 2024, the Russian state actor Midnight Blizzard (APT29) read the mailboxes of Microsoft's senior leadership, security and legal staff. The way in was not an exploit; it was a password spray against a forgotten legacy test account with no multi-factor authentication. This MSc analysis reconstructs the chain from primary disclosures and maps it to MITRE ATT&CK.

How a test account reached the corporate directory

The intrusion crossed a tenant boundary that identity architecture is supposed to hold. A legacy OAuth application in the compromised test tenant held privileged access into the corporate environment. The actor used it to register its own malicious applications and grant them the Exchange Online full_access_as_app role: access to every mailbox. All of it routed through residential proxies to blend in.

The decisive step has nothing to do with MFA. An application authenticates with a secret; no human is ever prompted. That is the core lesson of the whole case: in cloud identity, the application is often a weaker point than the person.

The ATT&CK mapping

The intrusion maps to 13 techniques across 10 tactics. A representative slice:

The finding that surprised me

Almost nothing in the chain was new. Of the ten techniques from spray to mail collection, seven already had APT29 entries in ATT&CK citing 2020–2022 reporting, three of them Microsoft's own publications. The adversary's playbook had been documented, by the victim, years before the first password was sprayed. Threat intelligence only works as a control when an organisation applies it to itself.

No endpoint, three log sources

The intrusion touched no endpoint at all. There was no malware to find, no host to image. Every trace sat in three identity and SaaS log sources: Entra ID sign-in logs, Entra audit logs, and the Microsoft 365 audit log. For a defender, the practical lesson is sharp: knowing which technique writes to which log matters more than recognising the technique's name. The rare events (a credential added to a dormant application, an app-role grant, the first sign-in of an application that had never signed in before) are nearly a signature if you're exporting the right logs.

Where the chain could have been cut

Public commentary fixed on the missing MFA. MFA on the test account would have stopped this spray, but it was the weakest control to rely on; any other route in would have reopened the same path. The control that fails closed is privilege: had the legacy application held no role-granting permission in the corporate tenant, the token abuse would have yielded nothing and the persistence steps could not have followed. The priority recommendations target application identity governance and monitoring, not user MFA alone:

  • Strip privileged application permissions; flag service principals owned by another tenant and remove ownerless ones.
  • Treat non-production tenants as production for identity: no dormant tenants, no test-tenant apps holding corporate permissions.
  • Scope mailbox access and retire legacy protocols; replace tenant-wide roles with per-mailbox application RBAC.
  • Alert on the rare events: credentials added to existing apps, app-role grants, first sign-in of a dormant application.

Why this one

I chose this incident because the victim is the identity platform most organisations depend on, so its failure modes are widely shared, and because the techniques are ones an analyst meets in any Microsoft 365 investigation. It's the clearest demonstration I found that modern intrusions are won and lost in identity logs, which is exactly where detection engineering has to live.
