Risk Quantification: Turning Threats into Numbers

Qualitative risk tells you something feels dangerous. Quantitative risk tells you how much it costs you per year — and sometimes the two disagree. This assessment is where my Quality background and GRC meet.

ProgrammeBSc · Cybersecurity Standards, Risk & Compliance
ScenarioA fictional mid-sized credit union
MethodQualitative + quantitative (SLE/ARO/ALE)
StandardsNIST SP 800-30 · ISO/IEC 27005

The brief

Assess the information-security risk to a fictional mid-sized credit union — roughly 10,000 members, a head office and several branches — across its three most critical systems: the core banking database, the Active Directory that authenticates everyone, and the member CRM. The goal wasn't just to rank them "high / medium / low"; it was to put a defensible annual figure on each, so the organisation could spend its next euro where it actually reduces the most risk.

Two lenses on the same risk

I assessed each asset twice. The qualitative pass rates impact on confidentiality, integrity and availability and the likelihood of compromise on a descriptive scale — the view that communicates well to a board. The quantitative pass converts that judgement into money using the standard model:

SLE × ARO = ALE — Single Loss Expectancy (the cost of one incident) multiplied by the Annual Rate of Occurrence (how many times a year you'd expect it) gives the Annual Loss Expectancy, a per-year exposure you can compare across very different assets.

The finding: impact and risk aren't the same thing

Qualitatively, the core banking database is the scariest asset — compromise it and you've hit confidentiality, integrity and availability at once, with immediate regulatory exposure. By impact alone, it's the clear number one.

But annualised exposure tells a different story. Active Directory credentials carry the highest ALE — €150,000 a year — even though a single breach costs less than the banking database. Why? Because credential-based attacks (phishing, password spraying, pass-the-hash) are so much more likely: an ARO of 0.30 against the database's 0.15. Frequency, not worst-case severity, drives the yearly cost.

So the spending priority isn't where intuition points. The money goes first to identity and access management — MFA, privileged-access controls and AD hardening — because that's where the largest annualised loss sits. The database still needs strong protection; it just isn't where the next euro does the most good.

Why this is the GRC skill that matters

Translating a fuzzy sense of danger into a number a decision-maker can act on is the core of governance, risk and compliance — and it's the same discipline I use every day in regulated-manufacturing Quality: quantify the risk, justify the control, and leave an auditable trail behind the decision. A risk register full of "highs" helps no one choose; an ALE ranking tells you exactly what to fund first.
